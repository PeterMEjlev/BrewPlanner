## About this file

This catalogue indexes Brülosophy experiments in which the tested variable directly concerns carbonation, packaging, package preparation, filling or closure, portable beer containers, packaging-stage oxygen control, packaging-stage antioxidants, or the storage of packaged beer. It follows the same format as the supplied reference catalogue and provides concise, original summaries rather than copies of the source articles.

**Scope:** Included experiments cover forced and natural carbonation, priming materials, spunding, kräusening, bottle and keg conditioning, burst carbonation, specialty carbonation gas, bottle fillers and closures, Crowlers, growlers, PET kegs, keg purging and transfer practices, antioxidants dosed at packaging, and temperature or light exposure after packaging. Experiments were excluded when packaging was merely part of the method and the tested variable concerned another process, such as dry-hop timing or fermentation pressure.

**Overlap:** Some entries also belong naturally in catalogues about oxygen and oxidation or clarification and conditioning. They are repeated here because the tested treatment is directly implemented during packaging, carbonation, or packaged storage.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better or identify the causal compounds. A non-significant result means the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest experiments and pandemic-era trials did not always use the later standardized multi-taster triangle-test format. Those entries explicitly describe single-brewer or adapted testing rather than presenting it as equivalent evidence.

The p-value field reproduces the value shown in Brülosophy’s master index. Where the underlying article materially differs from the index, the discrepancy is stated in the results section.

### [Natural Carbonation: Sucrose vs. Honey When Keg Conditioning A Belgian Single](https://brulosophy.com/2025/01/27/exbeeriment-natural-carbonation-sucrose-vs-honey-when-keg-conditioning-a-belgian-single/)

**Date:** 2025-01-27  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.038

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Belgian Single naturally carbonated in a keg with sucrose against the same beer keg conditioned with honey.

#### 2. Results

Twenty tasters participated, and exactly 11 identified the odd sample, meeting the significance threshold (p=0.038). Four correct tasters preferred the honey-conditioned beer, 2 preferred the sucrose beer, and 5 had no preference.

#### 3. Discussion

The priming substrate produced a detectable difference, although preference was mixed. Honey did not simply function as neutral fermentable sugar in this execution, but the result is specific to the honey, dose, beer, and conditioning period used.

### [Bottling Method: Blichmann BeerGun vs. Tapcooler Counter Pressure Bottle Filler In A Czech Dark Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2024/08/12/exbeeriment-bottling-method-blichmann-beergun-vs-tapcooler-counter-pressure-bottle-filler-in-a-czech-dark-lager/)

**Date:** 2024-08-12  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.58

#### 1. Purpose

Compare bottles filled from the same keg using a Blichmann BeerGun against bottles filled with a faucet-mounted Tapcooler counter-pressure filler.

#### 2. Results

After seven weeks of cold storage, 8 of 24 tasters correctly identified the odd sample, below the 13 required for significance (p=0.58). The brewer was correct in 3 of 5 attempts, perceived only a subtle possible mouthfeel difference, and detected no oxidation in either treatment.

### [Impact Flushing Bottles With CO2 Prior To Filling Has On A Cream Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2017/06/05/flushing-with-co2-vs-direct-filling-when-bottling-beer-from-kegs-exbeeriment-results/)

**Master-index p-value:** 0.38

#### 1. Purpose

Compare Cream Ale bottled from a keg with bottles first purged for 45 seconds using a BeerGun against beer filled through a picnic tap with no oxygen-control step.

#### 2. Results

Laboratory-style measurements showed much lower oxygen in BeerGun-filled bottles: roughly 26.5 to 43 ppb versus 250 to 700 ppb for the direct picnic-tap fills. Nevertheless, only 15 of 41 tasters identified the odd sample, below the 19 required for significance (p=0.38).

## Storage temperature and packaged-beer stability

### [Impact Storage Temperature Has On Bottled West Coast Pils](https://brulosophy.com/exbeeriments/exbeeriment-impact-storage-temperature-has-on-bottled-west-coast-pils/)

**Date:** 2024-09-23  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0002

#### 1. Purpose

Compare bottles of West Coast Pils stored for seven weeks at 45°F/7°C against identical bottles stored at 60°F/16°C.

#### 2. Results

Sixteen of 22 tasters correctly identified the odd sample (p=0.0002). Thirteen of the correct tasters preferred the cold-stored beer and 3 preferred the warm-stored version. The brewer found the warm beer soapy, muted, and harshly bitter.

#### 3. Discussion

Even a moderate 15°F/9°C storage difference substantially affected this hop-forward beer over seven weeks. The strong preference for the cold sample supports refrigerated storage, but the result remains specific to this package, oxygen exposure, and recipe.

#### 1. Purpose

Compare canned Hazy IPA stored for eight weeks at 38°F/3°C against the same canned beer stored at 68°F/20°C.

#### 2. Results

Exactly 12 of 21 tasters identified the odd sample, meeting the significance threshold (article p=0.021; master index p=0.02). Preference was mixed: 4 preferred the cold beer, 6 preferred the warm beer, and 2 expressed no clear preference. The warm beer also appeared hazier.

Warm storage produced a detectable difference but not the expected obvious oxidation failure, and more correct tasters preferred the warm sample. Temperature-related settling, carbonation, and chemical ageing may all have contributed, so the experiment does not isolate oxidation alone.
