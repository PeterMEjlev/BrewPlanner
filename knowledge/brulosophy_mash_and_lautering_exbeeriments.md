## About this file

This catalogue contains concise, original paraphrases of Brülosophy experiments in which the mash, mash chemistry, grain-bed handling, wort separation, sparging, or lautering method was the tested variable. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct source link and separate purpose, results, and discussion sections.

**Scope:** Included experiments directly test mash temperature, mash duration, step rests, decoction, mash pH adjustment, mash additives, oxygen-control methods applied during mashing, crush size, mash thickness, recirculation, mashout, mash-tun construction, sparging, vorlauf, BIAB handling, rice hulls, or the timing and extraction of roasted grain during mash or lauter. Ingredient comparisons and general boil, hop, fermentation, packaging, and equipment trials were excluded unless the manipulated variable was specifically part of mash or wort separation.

**Cross-listing:** Some experiments also appear in the Malt, Grain and Fermentables catalogue because the tested treatment concerns both the grain and how it was handled during mashing or lautering.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better or identify the causal flavour compounds. A non-significant result means that the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest experiments did not always use the later standardized triangle-test format. Those entries describe the comparative or informal method used rather than fabricating a p-value.

The p-value field reproduces Brülosophy’s master-index value. Where the master index omits a value or differs slightly from the linked article, the result summary states the article-level figure separately. The linked article title is used when it differs from the abbreviated master-index title.

## Mash temperature and wort fermentability

### [Mash Temperature: Low vs. High In A Saison](https://brulosophy.com/2024/11/04/exbeeriment-mash-temperature-low-vs-high-in-a-saison/)

**Date:** 2024-11-04  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.002

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Saison mashed at 148°F/64°C with the same recipe mashed at 164°F/73°C, with both beers fermented using a diastaticus Saison strain.

#### 2. Results

Both beers attenuated nearly completely, finishing at 1.001 and 1.002. Of 21 tasters, 14 correctly identified the odd sample, producing a significant result (p=0.002).

#### 3. Discussion

The beers were distinguishable despite the diastatic yeast largely erasing the expected final-gravity difference. Mash temperature therefore affected something beyond simple residual extract in this recipe, although the experiment does not identify whether aroma, flavour, or mouthfeel supplied the cue.

### [High vs. Low Mash Temperature In A Non-Alcoholic Hoppy Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2023/02/13/exbeeriment-high-vs-low-mash-temperature-in-a-non-alcoholic-hoppy-lager/)

**Date:** 2023-02-13  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.000004

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether a very high mash temperature could produce a perceptibly different non-alcoholic hoppy lager from the same recipe mashed at a conventional lower temperature.

#### 2. Results

The high-temperature beer finished at 1.007 and approximately 0.3% ABV, while the lower-temperature beer reached 1.001 and approximately 0.4% ABV. Fifteen of 17 tasters identified the odd sample (p=0.000004).

#### 3. Discussion

The high mash temperature materially changed the finished beer and retained more extract while keeping alcohol low. This supports high-temperature mashing as a useful formulation tool for non-alcoholic beer, but it does not show that the approach will work equally well with every grist, yeast, or hopping rate.

### [Mash Temperature: Low vs. High In A German Pils](https://brulosophy.com/2022/01/10/exbeeriment-mash-temperature-low-vs-high-in-a-german-pils/)

**Date:** 2022-01-10  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.21

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a German Pils mashed at a relatively low saccharification temperature with the same wort mashed at a substantially higher temperature.

#### 2. Results

The lower-temperature beer finished at 1.007, whereas the higher-temperature beer finished at 1.021. The COVID-era semi-blind evaluation did not reach significance (p=0.21).

#### 3. Discussion

A large attenuation difference did not translate into reliable sensory discrimination in this trial. Final gravity alone is therefore a poor predictor of whether a panel will perceive sweetness or body, although the single-person testing format limits the strength of the result.

### [Mash Temperature: Low vs. High In An English Porter](https://brulosophy.com/2020/06/15/mash-temperature-low-vs-high-in-an-english-porter-exbeeriment-results/)

## Mash duration and abbreviated schedules

### [Stacked Variables: Impact Reduced Mash & Boil Times Have On An American Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2023/05/22/exbeeriment-stacked-variables-impact-reduced-mash-boil-times-have-on-an-american-stout/)

**Date:** 2023-05-22  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.25

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Stout made with 30-minute mash and boil steps against the same recipe made with conventional 60-minute mash and boil steps.

#### 2. Results

The beers finished at 1.011 and 1.012. Ten of 24 tasters identified the odd sample, below the significance requirement (p=0.25), and the brewer was correct in only 2 of 5 semi-blind trials.

#### 3. Discussion

Halving both process steps did not create a reliably detectable difference in this stout. Because mash and boil duration changed together, the experiment cannot show whether either variable alone was neutral.

**Date:** 2022-10-31  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.038

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Brown Ale mashed for 60 minutes with the same recipe mashed for 120 minutes.

#### 2. Results

Both beers finished at 1.012. Eleven of 20 tasters identified the odd sample, exactly meeting the significance requirement (p=0.038).

#### 3. Discussion

Extending the mash produced a detectable difference without changing final gravity. The result narrowly crossed the threshold, so it should be treated as modest evidence and not as proof that a two-hour mash generally improves or harms beer.

### [Impact Reduced Mash Length Has On A Blonde Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2022/05/16/exbeeriment-impact-reduced-mash-length-has-on-a-blonde-ale/)

**Master-index p-value:** 0.45

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Blonde Ale mashed for 30 minutes with the same recipe mashed for 60 minutes.

#### 2. Results

The beers had nearly identical original and final gravities. Six of 16 tasters selected the odd sample, below the 9 required for significance (p=0.45).

#### 3. Discussion

A 30-minute mash was a practical time-saving substitute in this recipe. Conversion speed depends on malt, crush, temperature, mash chemistry, and equipment, so this result should not be applied blindly to every grist.

## Step mashes, decoctions and specialty rests

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Czech Dark Lager decocted with the roasted grains included against one where the roasted grains were excluded from the decoction.

#### 2. Results

Both beers finished at 1.014. Only 4 of 18 tasters identified the odd sample, producing a clearly non-significant result (p=0.90).

#### 3. Discussion

Including roasted grain in the decoction did not produce a reliable sensory difference under these conditions. The finding is recipe-specific and does not rule out effects with larger roasted-grain proportions or more intensive decoctions.

### [Impact Of A Single Decoction Mash When Using Under-Modified Malt In A Czech Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2025/12/01/exbeeriment-impact-of-a-single-decoction-mash-when-using-under-modified-malt-in-a-czech-pale-lager/)

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Czech Pale Lager made from under-modified malt using a single decoction mash against the same recipe made by single infusion.

#### 2. Results

The infusion beer finished at 1.015 and the decocted beer at 1.011. Sixteen of 27 tasters identified the odd sample, yielding a significant result (p=0.005).

The decoction produced a detectable difference when used with under-modified malt. This setup is more favourable to finding a decoction effect than trials using highly modified modern malt, but the experiment still does not establish which sensory compounds changed.

## Mash pH and acidification methods

### [Water Chemistry: Impact Increasing Mash pH With Slaked Lime Has On An Oatmeal Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2025/02/24/exbeeriment-water-chemistry-impact-increasing-mash-ph-with-slaked-lime-has-on-an-oatmeal-stout/)

**Date:** 2025-02-24  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.46

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an Oatmeal Stout whose mash pH was increased with slaked lime against an otherwise matched batch receiving no slaked lime.

#### 2. Results

The untreated beer finished at 1.018 and the slaked-lime beer at 1.015. The panel did not reliably distinguish the beers (p=0.46).

#### 3. Discussion

Raising mash pH with slaked lime did not create an obvious sensory difference in this dark beer. Roasted malt character may have masked subtle effects, and the result does not address extreme overdosing or mineral-balance errors.

### [Water Chemistry: Sauergut vs. Lactic Acid For Mash pH Adjustment In A Kölsch](https://brulosophy.com/2022/04/04/exbeeriment-water-chemistry-sauergut-vs-lactic-acid-for-mash-ph-adjustment-in-a-kolsch/)

**Master-index p-value:** 0.02

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Kölsch whose mash pH was adjusted with sauergut against the same recipe acidified with lactic acid.

#### 2. Results

The beers finished at 1.010 and 1.008. Exactly 12 of 21 tasters identified the odd sample (article p=0.021; master index p=0.02), and 8 of those 12 preferred the lactic-acid beer.

The two acidification methods produced distinguishable beer in this delicate style. Sauergut changes more than pH alone because it introduces biologically produced acids and fermentation-derived compounds.

### [Water Chemistry: Acidulated Malt vs. Phosphoric Acid In American Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2020/12/07/water-chemistry-phosphoric-acid-vs-acidulated-malt-in-american-pale-ale-exbeeriment-results/)

The two pH-adjustment approaches were sensory substitutes in this single-person trial. Acidulated malt also changes the grist, so equivalence should not be assumed at larger dosing rates.
