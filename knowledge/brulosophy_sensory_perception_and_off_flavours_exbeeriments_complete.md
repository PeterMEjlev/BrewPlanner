# Brülosophy exBEERiments: Sensory Perception and Off-Flavours

**Experiments included:** 12

**Source index:** https://brulosophy.com/projects/exbeeriments/

**Prepared:** 31 July 2026

## About this file

This catalogue contains concise, original paraphrases of individual Brülosophy articles concerning sensory perception, tasting bias, triangle-test behaviour, and deliberately dosed off-flavours. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct link to its source article and three sections: purpose, results, and discussion.

**Scope:** Included experiments directly manipulate expectation, visual appearance, participant sightedness, or knowledge of test performance; or compare a clean beer with the same beer deliberately dosed with a recognised flavour standard. General brewing-process experiments were excluded when they merely discussed the possibility of producing an off-flavour without directly testing sensory perception or an intentionally added standard.

**Overlap:** The compounds covered here can also relate to fermentation, hops, ingredient storage, wort handling, or contamination. They are retained in this catalogue because the tested variable was the sensory standard itself rather than the brewing process that might create or prevent it.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show that every taster could detect the difference, that the named compound was correctly identified, or that one sample was objectively better. A non-significant result means the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the samples were equivalent.

**Method caution:** Several perception experiments intentionally served identical samples, supplied misleading feedback, or combined separate cohorts. Their p-values do not always represent the conventional question of whether two genuinely different beers were distinguishable. The pandemic-era bitterness trial used repeated tests by one brewer rather than an independent panel. Each entry therefore describes the actual method rather than treating all results as equivalent evidence.

The p-value field reproduces the value shown in Brülosophy’s master index. Where an article contains several comparisons, no conventional discrimination test, or a value whose meaning is narrower than the title suggests, that limitation is stated in the results section.

## Expectation, feedback, sightedness and appearance

### [Impact Participant Sightedness Has On Triangle Test Performance](https://brulosophy.com/2022/07/18/exbeeriment-impact-participant-sightedness-has-on-triangle-test-performance/)

**Date:** 2022-07-18  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.31

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether telling participants which brewing variable had been changed, along with its expected effect, improved triangle-test performance compared with keeping participants blind to the variable.

#### 2. Results

The article reused two beer comparisons and collected separate blind and informed cohorts. In the dry-yeast preparation comparison, 6 of 25 blind tasters identified the odd sample (p=0.89), while 6 of 13 informed tasters did so (p=0.24). In the mash-temperature comparison, 12 of 33 blind tasters were correct (p=0.42), compared with 11 of 28 informed tasters (p=0.31). None of the four cohorts reached significance. The master-index value of 0.31 corresponds to the informed cohort in the mash-temperature comparison rather than a pooled test of sightedness.

#### 3. Discussion

Informing tasters of the variable and its presumed effect did not produce a detectable improvement in either comparison. This weakens the claim that advance knowledge routinely helps tasters focus on subtle differences. However, the trial used different people in each cohort and only two underlying beer comparisons, so it does not prove that sightedness can never affect performance. The practical argument for maintaining blindness remains that it limits an avoidable source of expectation bias without showing a clear performance penalty here.

### [Impact Knowledge Of Triangle Test Performance Has On Level Of Confidence And Degree Of Difference Perceived](https://brulosophy.com/2022/06/20/exbeeriment-impact-knowledge-of-triangle-test-performance-has-on-level-of-confidence-and-degree-of-difference-perceived/)

**Date:** 2022-06-20  
**Master-index p-value:** N/A

#### 1. Purpose

Determine whether telling tasters that their triangle-test selection was correct, incorrect, or providing no feedback changed their reported confidence and the size of the difference they believed they had perceived.

#### 2. Results

All 29 participants received three samples of the same Blonde Ale. Ten selected the red cup, 10 selected blue, and 9 selected green, as expected when no sample was actually unique. The red group was falsely told it was correct, the blue group was told it was incorrect, and the green group received no performance feedback. Being told one was correct did not significantly change confidence relative to receiving no feedback (p=0.18). Being told one was incorrect produced lower confidence than receiving no feedback (p=0.006) and lower confidence than being told one was correct (p=0.038). Reported degree of difference did not differ significantly among the three feedback conditions, with pairwise p-values of 0.48, 0.11, and 0.08.

#### 3. Discussion

Post-test feedback altered confidence even though every sample was identical and the feedback was arbitrary. The clearest statistical result was not that praise elevated confidence, but that being told the selection was wrong reduced it. Feedback did not reliably change the magnitude of difference participants claimed to perceive. The small groups and deceptive design make this exploratory rather than definitive, but the result demonstrates why confidence and sensory accuracy should not be treated as interchangeable.

### [Impact Appearance Has On Perception Of Other Beer Qualities](https://brulosophy.com/2017/04/27/the-impact-appearance-has-on-perception-of-other-beer-qualities-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-04-27  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.20

#### 1. Purpose

Assess whether changing only the apparent colour of a Blonde Ale altered how tasters described its hop, malt, fermentation, dryness, and style characteristics.

#### 2. Results

Half of one Blonde Ale was darkened to roughly 33 SRM with 136 g of Sinamar. Twenty-eight conference attendees evaluated the pale and dark versions while able to see them, rating intensity and selecting descriptors. Their responses often shifted in directions associated with colour, including darker-beer malt impressions, although the article did not report inferential statistics for those survey distributions. A separate blindfolded triangle-test dataset was collected to test whether Sinamar itself created a detectable flavour difference. Across 15 participants, 7 identified the odd sample, below the 9 required for significance (p=0.20). The brewer also failed a blind triangle test after initially perceiving obvious chocolate and roast notes when looking at the dark beer.

#### 3. Discussion

The sighted descriptions suggest appearance shaped what tasters believed they smelled and tasted, while the blindfolded data failed to establish that the beers were sensorially different without visual information. The evidence is suggestive rather than cleanly causal because the main side-by-side survey lacked inferential analysis, participant behaviour at a seminar was difficult to control, and Sinamar cannot simply be assumed to be perfectly flavourless at a large dose. Even with those limitations, the contrast between sighted descriptions and blind discrimination provides a useful warning against treating tasting notes as independent of visual context.

### [The Impact of Expectation on Perception](https://brulosophy.com/2014/11/03/the-impact-of-expectation-on-perception-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2014-11-03  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.94

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether the expectation that one sample was different could lead tasters to perceive differences among three samples that were all poured from the same keg.

#### 2. Results

Ten participants received three identical samples and were presented with a survey that implied one might be different, while still offering “no detectable differences” as an option. Only 1 participant selected the no-difference response; the other 9 confidently identified one of the identical samples as different. The master index lists p=0.94, but this was an intentional deception exercise rather than a conventional triangle test containing a genuinely unique sample, so the value should not be interpreted like the discrimination p-values used elsewhere in the catalogue.

#### 3. Discussion

The trial illustrates how task framing can manufacture perceived differences and confidence among experienced brewers and beer servers. It does not estimate how often expectation bias occurs in ordinary tasting, because the sample was very small, there was no control group, and participants were intentionally primed. Its practical value is methodological: sensory claims require blinding, replication, and a statistical threshold precisely because sincere tasters can perceive distinctions that are not physically present.

## Sulfur and fermentation-derived off-flavours

### [Off-Flavor: Dimethyl Sulfide (DMS) In A Dark Mild](https://brulosophy.com/2023/08/07/exbeeriment-off-flavor-dimethyl-sulfide-dms-in-a-dark-mild/)

**Date:** 2023-08-07  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.39

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Dark Mild dosed with dimethyl sulfide flavour standard against an otherwise identical, non-dosed sample to test whether DMS remained detectable in a dark, malt-forward beer.

#### 2. Results

The beer finished at 1.011. Half was dosed to approximately 100 µg/L DMS, around twice the cited detection threshold of 25 to 50 µg/L. Forty-seven Homebrew Con attendees received two dosed samples and one control sample in opaque cups. Seventeen selected the control as unique, below the 22 required for significance (p=0.39). The brewer identified the odd sample in all 5 personal tests, but described the dosed beer as having a slight metallic twang rather than the expected creamed-corn or cooked-vegetable character.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel did not reliably distinguish DMS at the chosen dose in this Dark Mild, despite the nominal concentration being above the cited threshold. The malt-rich base may have masked the compound, the stated threshold may not transfer cleanly across beers, or sensitivity may vary substantially among people. The brewer’s metallic description also shows that detecting a difference does not guarantee correct identification of the responsible compound.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Acetaldehyde In A Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2017/02/06/off-flavor-series-acetaldehyde-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-02-06  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0009

#### 1. Purpose

Test whether blind tasters could distinguish a clean pale lager dosed with acetaldehyde flavour standard from the same lager without the standard, and whether correct tasters could identify the compound.

#### 2. Results

Bitburger was dosed with FlavorActiV acetaldehyde standard at approximately three times the stated flavour threshold. Of 22 participants, 15 identified the dosed sample, exceeding the 12 required for significance (p=0.0009). Eleven of the correct tasters preferred the clean lager and 4 had no preference; none preferred the dosed beer. All 15 correctly selected which of the two beers contained an off-flavour, but only 6 named acetaldehyde. Other selections included DMS, astringency, metallic, butyric acid, and oxidation. The brewer repeatedly distinguished the dosed sample, initially through mouthfeel and later through a bug-spray-like aroma rather than a simple green-apple note.

#### 3. Discussion

Acetaldehyde at the training dose caused a reliable sensory difference and was rejected by most correct tasters. Identification was much weaker than discrimination, with fewer than half of the correct tasters naming the compound even after receiving descriptors. The result therefore supports detectability at a high dose, not the assumption that judges can reliably diagnose acetaldehyde at lower concentrations in an independently evaluated beer.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Diacetyl In A Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2017/01/02/off-flavor-series-pt-2-diacetyl-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-01-02  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.002

#### 1. Purpose

Determine whether blind tasters could distinguish a pale lager dosed with diacetyl standard from the same undosed lager and correctly identify the off-flavour.

#### 2. Results

Bitburger was dosed with two FlavorActiV capsules, estimated by the article to yield roughly six times the cited threshold. Thirteen of 19 participants identified the unique sample, exceeding the 11 required for significance (p=0.002). Nine correct tasters preferred the clean beer, 3 preferred the dosed beer, and 1 had no preference. Only 4 of the 13 correct tasters identified diacetyl from the supplied list; others selected chlorophenol, oxidation, metallic, or other options. The brewer personally identified the unique sample in only 2 of 6 repeated tests, equivalent to chance, and did not perceive the buttery character he expected.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel established that the high diacetyl dose changed the beer, but most correct tasters still failed to diagnose the compound. The brewer’s chance-level performance further demonstrates large individual differences in sensitivity or recognition. A triangle test asks whether samples differ, while beer judging asks a taster to identify and describe a fault without a matched control. This experiment supports the first ability under these conditions much more strongly than the second.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Dimethyl Sulfide (DMS) In A Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2016/09/05/off-flavor-series-pt-1-dimethyl-sulfide-dms-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2016-09-05  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.77

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether a group containing several trained or experienced beer tasters could distinguish Bitburger dosed above the stated DMS threshold from undosed samples of the same beer.

#### 2. Results

Eleven participants received one DMS-dosed sample and two controls. Seven correct responses were required for significance, but only 3 participants selected the dosed beer (p=0.77). After being told that an off-flavour was present, only one person identified DMS, and that person had selected the correct triangle sample. The brewer found the dosed beer easy to identify in repeated tests, describing boiled cabbage and ketchup rather than creamed corn. A deliberate all-control trick during one blind sequence initially caused him to doubt his perception, but he subsequently detected the dosed sample when it was actually presented.

#### 3. Discussion

This small, experienced panel did not reliably detect the DMS treatment, contradicting the expectation that the compound is universally obvious above threshold. The brewer and two other repeat performers suggest that individual sensitivity may differ, but the study did not quantify concentration analytically or test each participant repeatedly. The result is a caution against assuming that familiarity with off-flavour terminology guarantees either detection or accurate identification.

## Organic acids, bitterness and astringency

### [Off-Flavor: Isovaleric Acid In An International Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2023/10/16/exbeeriment-off-flavor-isovaleric-acid-in-an-international-pale-lager/)

**Date:** 2023-10-16  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0009

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an International Pale Lager dosed with isovaleric acid flavour standard against the same beer without the standard, focusing on the detectability and preference impact of the cheesy, sweaty character associated with the compound.

#### 2. Results

After six weeks of conditioning, one portion was dosed to approximately 2.4 mg/L, more than twice the article’s cited 1 mg/L threshold. Fourteen of 20 participants identified the unique sample, exceeding the 11 required for significance (p=0.0009). The article subsequently refers to 13 correct participants when introducing the preference survey, an internal numerical inconsistency. The reported preferences total 14 responses: 3 preferred the dosed beer, 8 preferred the clean beer, and 3 had no preference. The brewer was correct in all 5 personal tests and perceived a faint funky, cheesy aroma rather than an overwhelmingly offensive character.

#### 3. Discussion

Isovaleric acid at slightly more than twice the cited threshold produced a reliable difference, although not everyone detected it and a minority preferred the dosed beer. This reinforces the distinction between detectability and dislike, even for a compound conventionally labelled an off-flavour. The article’s mismatch between the number of correct tasters and the preference-survey introduction should be kept in mind, although it does not change the reported triangle-test significance.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Bitterness](https://brulosophy.com/2020/08/31/off-flavor-series-bitterness-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-08-31  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.003

#### 1. Purpose

Test whether a small increase in bitterness, created by dosing Miller Lite with an iso-alpha-acid flavour standard, was perceptible relative to the undosed beer.

#### 2. Results

The author used one third of a FlavorActiV capsule, estimating that it increased the beer by approximately 5 IBU, although this estimate was not confirmed analytically. Pandemic restrictions prevented normal panel testing, so the author completed 10 semi-blind triangle tests using randomized marked cups. He identified the unique sample 8 times, exceeding the 7 required for significance in this repeated single-person design (p=0.003). He perceived a subtle lingering sensation after swallowing the dosed beer and slightly preferred it, but was not confident that he would have labelled the sensation as bitterness without knowing the treatment.

#### 3. Discussion

The repeated tests show that one brewer could distinguish the dosed and undosed beers, but they do not establish population-level sensitivity to a 5 IBU difference. The actual IBU change was inferred from assumptions about the flavour standard rather than measured, and expectation could not be eliminated fully in a single-author semi-blind design. The result is useful as an individual demonstration, not strong evidence for a universal 5 IBU discrimination threshold.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Astringency In A Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2017/12/21/off-flavor-series-astringent-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-12-21  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.65

#### 1. Purpose

Determine whether blind tasters could distinguish a pale-lager blend dosed with an astringency flavour standard from an otherwise identical clean blend, and whether those detecting a difference could name the sensation.

#### 2. Results

The base consisted of equal parts Miller High Life and Miller Lite, with one portion dosed according to the FlavorActiV training recommendation. Nineteen participants completed the triangle test. Six identified the unique sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.65). Of those 6, only 4 subsequently selected the correct beer as the dosed one. None identified astringency from the supplied list; responses instead included diacetyl, oxidation, metallic, acetaldehyde, and chlorophenol. The brewer found the difference slight, eventually associating the dosed beer with sharper bitterness and a fuzzy, prickly oral sensation.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel did not reliably detect the astringency standard, and even the few correct triangle-test participants did not diagnose it. Astringency is a tactile drying or puckering sensation that can be confused with bitterness and other forms of harshness, which may explain the poor identification. The experiment does not show that tannin-driven astringency is imaginary or never detectable; it shows that this particular standard, dose, beer matrix, and panel did not support confident discrimination or labelling.

### [FlavorActiV Off-Flavor Series: Butyric Acid In A Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2017/05/25/off-flavor-series-butyric-acid-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-05-25  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.049

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether blind tasters could distinguish Miller High Life dosed with butyric acid standard from clean samples and correctly identify the compound associated with vomit-like, rancid, or cheesy character.

#### 2. Results

The dosed beer received FlavorActiV standard at approximately three times the stated threshold. Nine of 16 participants selected the unique sample, exactly meeting the requirement for significance (p=0.049). Eight of the 9 correct tasters preferred the clean beer, while 1 preferred the dosed beer. All 9 correctly identified which beer contained an off-flavour after the triangle test, but only 3 named butyric acid; 2 selected isovaleric acid, 2 DMS, 1 diacetyl, and 1 lightstruck. The brewer consistently distinguished the dosed beer and identified it as butyric acid from its baby-vomit or stomach-acid character.

#### 3. Discussion

The result barely crossed the conventional significance threshold, so the evidence of discrimination is real but not overwhelming. Strong rejection among correct tasters supports the conventional view that butyric character is generally undesirable, while the single preference for the dosed beer demonstrates that preference is not universal. Most correct tasters still failed to name the compound, often confusing it with isovaleric acid or unrelated faults, again separating the ability to notice a difference from the ability to diagnose it.
