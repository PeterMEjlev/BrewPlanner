# Brülosophy exBEERiments: Flavour Additions and Speciality Ingredients

**Experiments included:** 63

**Source index:** https://brulosophy.com/projects/exbeeriments/

**Prepared:** 31 July 2026

## About this file

This catalogue contains concise, original paraphrases of individual Brülosophy articles concerning flavour additions and speciality ingredients. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct link to its source article and three sections: purpose, results, and discussion.

**Scope:** Included experiments directly test coffee, cacao, fruit, vegetables, herbs, spices, botanicals, wood, smoke, colouring or flavour products, unusual beverage bases, sugars, syrups, honey, alternative sweeteners, or acids and salt added primarily to alter finished flavour. Ordinary base-malt and cereal comparisons were excluded unless the ingredient was distinctly speciality-oriented, such as smoked malt, peated malt, koji rice or oat milk.

**Overlap:** Some entries also belong naturally in other catalogues. Examples include Phantasm and Tropic Thunder in hopping, salt and acid additions in water chemistry, fruit and sugar trials in cider, and priming sugars in packaging and carbonation. They are retained here because the identity, timing or format of the flavouring ingredient is itself the tested variable.

**Exclusions:** General enzymes, yeast nutrients, finings, antioxidants and processing aids were excluded when their primary purpose was fermentation performance, clarification, oxidation control or process efficiency rather than flavour addition. An experiment conducted in a flavoured beer was also excluded when the flavouring ingredient was held constant and an unrelated variable was tested.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better, quantify the practical size of the difference or identify the causal flavour compounds. A non-significant result means that the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest experiments and several pandemic-era trials did not use the later standard independent-panel format. Those entries describe the actual comparative, informal or repeated single-brewer method rather than presenting it as equivalent evidence.

The p-value field follows Brülosophy’s master index. Where the linked article reports a more precise value or uses a different test design, that detail is stated in the results section.

## Coffee, cacao, fruit and vegetable additions

### [Brewing With Coffee: Store-Bought vs. Home Roasted Beans In A Cream Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2024/12/30/exbeeriment-brewing-with-coffee-store-bought-vs-home-roasted-beans-in-a-cream-ale/)

**Date:** 2024-12-30  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.01

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Cream Ale flavoured with commercially roasted coffee beans against the same beer made with beans roasted at home.

#### 2. Results

Of 24 tasters, 14 correctly identified the odd sample, exceeding the 13 required for significance (p=0.01). The brewer was correct in all five semi-blind trials, describing the commercial roast as bolder and more bitter and the home roast as subtler; he preferred the commercial coffee beer.

#### 3. Discussion

The coffee source and roasting process produced a detectable difference in this Cream Ale. Because roast degree, bean condition and roasting equipment changed together, the trial cannot isolate whether home roasting itself or the resulting roast profile caused the distinction.

### [Brewing With Coffee: Central American vs. Indonesian Coffee In A White Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2024/01/15/exbeeriment-brewing-with-coffee-central-american-vs-indonesian-coffee-in-a-white-stout/)

**Date:** 2024-01-15  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.34

#### 1. Purpose

Compare otherwise matched White Stouts flavoured with Central American or Indonesian coffee.

#### 2. Results

Of 20 tasters, 8 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.34). The brewer failed all five semi-blind trials and described both White Stouts as similarly pungent, coffee-forward beers with clean malt character and soft mouthfeel.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel did not reliably distinguish the Central American and Indonesian coffees at the tested dose and extraction method. Coffee origin may matter more with different roast levels, larger additions or a less flavourful base beer, so the result is not evidence that origins are generally interchangeable.

### [Impact Pureeing Fruit Has On A Hard Cider](https://brulosophy.com/2023/07/03/exbeeriment-impact-pureeing-fruit-has-on-a-hard-cider/)

**Date:** 2023-07-03  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.36

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether pureeing the fruit before adding it to hard cider changes the finished beverage compared with using the fruit without pureeing.

#### 2. Results

Of 26 tasters, 10 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 14 required for significance (p=0.36). The pureed-fruit cider stabilised about a week sooner, while final gravities were nearly identical at 1.002 and 1.001; the brewer identified the odd sample once in five attempts and found both similarly peach-forward.

#### 3. Discussion

Pureeing accelerated extraction and produced more persistent haze, plausibly from pectin and suspended fruit material, but did not create a reliably detectable blind sensory difference. The practical trade-off was therefore process speed and clarity rather than demonstrated flavour intensity.

### [Adding Fruit Before vs. After Fermentation In A Cherry Hard Cider](https://brulosophy.com/2023/05/01/exbeeriment-adding-fruit-before-vs-after-fermentation-in-a-cherry-hard-cider/)

**Date:** 2023-05-01  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0000002

#### 1. Purpose

Compare cherry hard cider made by adding the fruit before fermentation against cider receiving the fruit after fermentation.

#### 2. Results

Of 20 tasters, 18 correctly identified the odd sample, producing a highly significant result (p=0.0000002). Among correct tasters, 7 preferred fruit added before fermentation and 9 preferred fruit added after fermentation, while the brewer was correct in all five trials and described the pre-fermentation version as crisper and the post-fermentation version as sweeter and fuller.

#### 3. Discussion

Fermenting the cherry addition materially changed the cider compared with adding fruit after primary fermentation. Yeast metabolism, sugar retention and extraction conditions all differed, so the trial demonstrates a treatment effect without isolating the exact chemical mechanism.

### [Brewing With Coffee: Revisiting Hot vs. Cold Brew In Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2020/12/14/brewing-with-coffee-revisiting-hot-vs-cold-brew-in-stout-exbeeriment-results/)

**Master-index p-value:** 0.896

#### 1. Purpose

Revisit whether adding hot-brewed or cold-brewed coffee produces a perceptible difference in Stout.

#### 2. Results

Pandemic restrictions limited the evaluation to 10 semi-blind triangle tests by the brewer, who identified the odd sample only twice (p=0.896). He found the hot-extracted and cold-extracted coffee treatments perceptibly identical, with both contributing cocoa, cinnamon and restrained bitterness.

This repeat did not reproduce a detectable hot-versus-cold coffee difference. The evidence is weak because it came from one taster, and differences in recipe, dose, coffee and extraction procedure may explain the contrast with other coffee trials.

### [Frozen Fruit vs. Tincture In A Belgian Dubbel With Plums](https://brulosophy.com/2020/10/05/frozen-fruit-vs-tincture-in-a-belgian-dubble-with-plums-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-10-05  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0003

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Belgian Dubbel flavoured with frozen plums against one dosed with a plum tincture.

#### 2. Results

In 10 semi-blind triangle tests, the brewer correctly identified the odd sample every time (p<0.0003). The frozen-plum beer finished at 1.004 and the tincture beer at 1.003; he described the tincture as sharper and the fruit treatment as rounder and more jam-like, while both clearly expressed plum.

#### 3. Discussion

Whole frozen fruit and an alcohol tincture produced an obvious difference in this Dubbel. Ethanol extraction, fruit skins, pulp and added liquid all changed together, so the result cannot attribute the distinction solely to the use of a tincture.

### [Brewing With Coffee: Boil Kettle vs. Fermentation Vessel Addition In A Cream Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2019/01/21/brewing-with-coffee-boil-kettle-addition-vs-fermentation-vessel-addition-exbeeriment-results/)

**Master-index p-value:** 0.003

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Cream Ale receiving coffee in the boil kettle against one receiving the coffee in the fermentation vessel.

#### 2. Results

Of 28 tasters, 17 correctly identified the odd sample, exceeding the 15 required for significance (p=0.003). Preference was nearly even, with 7 favouring the boil addition and 8 the fermentation-vessel addition; the brewer consistently found the boil-treated beer harsher and more bitter, while the cold-side addition retained richer and smoother coffee character.

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American IPA receiving rosemary with 30 minutes left in the boil against one receiving rosemary at flameout.

#### 2. Results

#### 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether replacing 10% of the grist with koji rice produces a perceptible difference in an International Pale Lager.

#### 2. Results

Of 26 tasters, 12 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 14 required for significance (p=0.12). Both Belgian Golden Strong Ales finished at 1.008; the brewer was correct in 3 of 5 trials and found both similarly spicy, lightly alcoholic and grape- or white-wine-like.

#### 3. Discussion

Adding candi syrup during the boil or at high kräusen produced similar attenuation and no reliable panel difference. With a healthy pitch and controlled fermentation, the theoretical yeast-stress advantage of the later addition did not yield a demonstrated benefit.

Compare a Belgian Golden Strong Ale receiving all sugar at the start of fermentation against one receiving three staggered additions.

#### 2. Results

Of 37 tasters, 16 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 18 required for significance (p=0.10). The single-dose beer finished at 1.004 and the three-addition beer at 1.005; both fermented successfully, and the panel did not reliably distinguish them.

## General limitations

These experiments are useful controlled brewing comparisons, but they are not replications across breweries, ingredient lots, recipes or panels. Most involve one brew day, one recipe, one treatment level and a convenience sample of tasters.

Triangle tests assess whether a panel can identify an odd sample more often than expected by chance. They do not measure effect size, show that two treatments are equivalent after a non-significant result or establish which beer is better. Preference responses from only the tasters who correctly completed a significant triangle test are descriptive, not a general preference poll.

Brewer impressions can add useful detail but are vulnerable to expectation, especially when the brewer knew the tested variable. Pandemic-era repeated tests performed by one brewer provide substantially weaker evidence than independent multi-person panels.

Ingredient additions are especially difficult to generalise. Variety, freshness, processing, dose, extraction method, contact time, base beer, fermentation and serving conditions can all change the outcome. The linked source article should be consulted for the complete recipe, procedure, photographs and authors’ full commentary.
