**Experiments included:** 39

## About this file

This catalogue contains concise, original paraphrases of Brülosophy experiments where brewing equipment, vessel material, vessel geometry, container size, filtration hardware, filling equipment, closures, or serving glass treatment was the tested variable. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct source link and separate purpose, results, and discussion sections.

**Scope:** Included experiments directly compare fermentation vessels, fermenter dimensions or openness, mash-tun and chilling equipment, hop-containment devices, mechanical filters, bottle fillers, storage containers, bottle closures, or glass-cleaning treatments. Trials where only pressure, temperature, carbonation rate, transfer timing, or another process setting changed were excluded unless the physical vessel or device was central to the comparison.

**Cross-listing:** Several entries also appear in the Yeast and Fermentation, Hops and Hopping, Mash and Lautering, or packaging-related catalogues because the same experiment concerns both a process area and the equipment used to perform it.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better or identify the causal flavour compounds. A non-significant result means that the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest equipment trials did not always compare finished beer using the later standardized triangle-test format. Those entries describe the performance comparison or informal observation actually reported rather than fabricating a p-value.

The p-value field reproduces Brülosophy’s master-index value. Where the master index rounds a value or the linked article gives a more precise figure, the result summary states the article-level value separately.

## Fermentation vessel materials and construction

### [Fermentation Vessel: HDPE Bucket vs. PET Carboy In A Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2020/11/23/fermentation-vessel-hdpe-bucket-vs-pet-carboy-in-a-pale-ale-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-11-23  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.21

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Pale Ale fermented in a food-grade HDPE bucket against the same wort fermented in a PET carboy.

#### 2. Results

Both beers reached the same original and final gravities, finishing at 1.005. Under pandemic restrictions, the brewer completed ten semi-blind triangle tests and identified the odd sample 5 times, below the 7 required for significance (p=0.21). Differences he thought he noticed at packaging had disappeared after conditioning, and he described the beers as identical.

#### 3. Discussion

The article found no detectable penalty from using the inexpensive bucket in this short fermentation. It contrasts this with the significant 2014 bucket-versus-PET result and proposes possible explanations such as the old bucket used in the first trial, flavour carryover, individual sensory threshold, or panel differences. Long storage remains a separate concern because HDPE is more oxygen-permeable.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Glass Carboy vs. Plastic Speidel Tank In A German Pils](https://brulosophy.com/2018/01/01/fermentation-vessel-pt-6-glass-carboy-vs-plastic-speidel-tank-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2018-01-01  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.70

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a German Pils fermented in a glass carboy against the same beer fermented in a thick-walled HDPE Speidel tank.

#### 2. Results

Fermentation progressed similarly and both beers finished at 1.010. Of 20 tasters, 6 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.70). The brewer was correct in 3 of 8 trials and reported no detectable difference in aroma, flavour, or mouthfeel.

#### 3. Discussion

The article uses this non-significant result to question simple explanations based only on vessel geometry or material. It notes that the non-significant vessel comparisons involved relatively new, low-permeability containers, while earlier significant trials involved more permeable or well-used plastic. These are hypotheses generated by a mixed series of results, not mechanisms established by this experiment.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Stainless Ss Brew Bucket vs. Plastic Speidel Tank In An American Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2017/10/23/fermentation-vessel-pt-5-stainless-ss-brew-bucket-vs-plastic-speidel-tank-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-10-23  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.70

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Pale Ale fermented in a stainless Ss Brew Bucket against the same beer fermented in a plastic Speidel tank.

#### 2. Results

Both beers fermented similarly and reached the same 1.012 final gravity. Of 20 tasters, only 6 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 11 required (p=0.70). The brewer identified 2 of 7 trials and another contributor also failed, with both describing the beers as effectively identical.

#### 3. Discussion

The result did not support a detectable difference between these two relatively impermeable, easy-transfer vessels. In discussing why earlier vessel tests were significant, the article raises oxygen ingress and flavour carryover from heavily used plastic as more plausible than material alone. Practical durability and cleaning, not sensory performance, drove the brewer's preference for stainless.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Corny Keg vs. Glass Carboy In An American Amber Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2017/02/13/fermentation-vessel-pt-4-corny-keg-vs-glass-carboy-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-02-13  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.01

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Amber Ale fermented in a tall stainless Cornelius keg against the same beer fermented in a wider glass carboy, while using pressure transfer for both to reduce packaging differences.

#### 2. Results

The keg beer finished at 1.012 and the carboy beer at 1.013. Of 29 tasters, 16 correctly identified the odd sample, exceeding the 15 required (article p=0.013; master index rounded to 0.01). Six preferred the carboy beer, 3 preferred the keg beer, 3 had no preference, and 4 later reported no perceived difference. The brewer himself identified only 1 of 3 trials and considered the beers too similar to distinguish.

#### 3. Discussion

The article regarded the significant panel result as evidence that vessel shape, surface area, yeast contact, headspace, light exposure, or another correlated factor may influence beer. It did not identify which factor was responsible. Preference leaned toward glass, but the brewer emphasized that this did not make keg fermentation objectively worse and continued using kegs for their process advantages.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Stainless Steel vs. Glass Carboy In An American IPA](https://brulosophy.com/2016/07/11/fermentation-vessel-pt-3-stainless-steel-vs-glass-carboy-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2016-07-11  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.81

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American IPA fermented in a stainless steel Brew Bucket against the same wort fermented in a glass carboy.

#### 2. Results

Nineteen people participated. Only 5 identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.81). The stainless-fermented beer appeared clearer at packaging, but the panel could not reliably distinguish the beers by sensory evaluation.

#### 3. Discussion

The article found no support for a sensory effect from stainless versus glass in this execution, despite significant results in the two preceding vessel experiments. It considered geometry, volume, material, oxygen permeability, and ordinary experimental variability as possible explanations for the inconsistent series. The result supports choosing between these vessels primarily on safety and convenience for similar short fermentations.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Plastic (PET) Carboy vs. Glass Carboy In A California Common](https://brulosophy.com/2016/05/16/fermentor-type-pt-2-plastic-pet-vs-glass-carboy-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2016-05-16  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.02

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a California Common fermented in a PET carboy against the same wort fermented in a glass carboy.

#### 2. Results

Both beers followed similar fermentation schedules and finished at 1.009. Of 25 tasters, 14 correctly identified the odd sample, exceeding the 13 required (p=0.016, rounded to 0.02 in the index). Ten preferred the glass-fermented beer, 2 preferred PET, 1 reported no preference, and 1 reported no difference. The brewer repeatedly performed near chance and could not personally distinguish them.

#### 3. Discussion

The article viewed this as a second surprising indication that vessel choice could influence finished beer, with oxygen permeability offered as one plausible explanation. However, participants had little confidence, the brewer could not discriminate, and the beers appeared extremely similar in ordinary serving. The result identifies a panel-level difference but not its mechanism or practical magnitude.

### [Fermentation Vessel: Plastic (PET) Carboy vs. Bucket In An American IPA](https://brulosophy.com/2014/10/14/the-impact-of-fermentation-vessel-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2014-10-14  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.007

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American IPA produced from one wort and one yeast pitch, split between a PET carboy and a plastic bucket as the only planned fermentation variable.

#### 2. Results

Ten blind tasters participated in the early triangle test. Seven correctly identified the odd sample (p=0.007). Most correct tasters considered the beers similar in appearance and mouthfeel, but 4 of 7 preferred the carboy-fermented beer; only one reported no detectable difference. After reviewing the results, the brewer also perceived a subtle difference despite not expecting one.

#### 3. Discussion

The article treated the finding as surprising evidence that vessel choice might affect beer, while warning against selecting equipment from this small trial alone. Potential causes such as oxygen permeability, geometry, headspace, or the condition and history of the bucket were not isolated. The small panel makes this an important prompt for replication rather than a definitive ranking of PET and bucket fermentation.

## Vessel size, volume, headspace and open fermentation

### [Impact Open Fermentation Has On A Festbier](https://brulosophy.com/2025/01/13/exbeeriment-impact-open-fermentation-has-on-a-festbier/)

**Date:** 2025-01-13  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.34

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Festbier fermented under a loose foil covering with the same beer fermented in a sealed vessel with a blow-off tube.

#### 2. Results

The open fermenter was sealed after one week, and the finished beers showed only a slight gravity difference. Of 20 tasters, 8 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.34). The brewer could not consistently distinguish them and considered both good Festbiers.

#### 3. Discussion

At this homebrew scale, open versus sealed fermentation did not produce a reliable sensory difference or obvious contamination. The result may reflect the small pressure difference between the vessels and the behaviour of Imperial Yeast L13 Global. Because open fermentation adds an avoidable contamination pathway without a demonstrated benefit in this trial, the author preferred to continue using sealed vessels.

### [Impact Fermenter Size Has On An American Brown Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2022/09/19/exbeeriment-impact-fermenter-size-has-on-an-american-brown-ale/)

**Date:** 2022-09-19  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.08

#### 1. Purpose

Compare identical volumes of American Brown Ale fermented in a 6 gallon/23 liter vessel and a 13 gallon/49 liter vessel. The larger fermenter created about 7.25 gallons/27 liters of headspace, compared with roughly 0.25 gallons/0.95 liters in the smaller vessel.

#### 2. Results

Twenty-seven tasters participated. Thirteen identified the beer from the larger vessel, one fewer than the 14 required for significance (p=0.08). The brewer failed all five semi-blind triangle tests and described both beers as identical, with chocolate and caramel malt balanced by moderate pine-like hops.

#### 3. Discussion

The article interprets the result as evidence that neither the large difference in vessel size nor the associated headspace difference produced a reliably perceptible effect in this Brown Ale. It also notes that vessel shape differed and contrasts the result with earlier headspace work, so it does not establish that fermenter geometry or headspace can never matter. The brewer saw no reason to avoid making smaller batches in oversized vessels.

### [Impact Open Fermentation Has On An American Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2022/08/15/exbeeriment-impact-open-fermentation-has-on-an-american-pale-ale/)

**Date:** 2022-08-15  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.69

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Pale Ale fermented in a sealed vessel with the same wort fermented in an open vessel, using Imperial Yeast I22 Capri and otherwise matching the process.

#### 2. Results

The open-fermented beer finished at 1.018, four gravity points above the sealed beer at 1.014. Of 23 tasters, 7 identified the odd sample, below the 12 required for significance (p=0.69). The brewer identified it in 3 of 5 semi-blind trials and found both beers similarly juicy and Cascade-forward.

#### 3. Discussion

The article notes that open fermentation had been detectable in more yeast-driven Saison and British ale trials but not in this Pale Ale or a Czech lager. It proposes that yeast strain may determine the effect, or that the hop character may have masked smaller fermentation differences. Given the contamination and oxidation risks and the lack of a sensory result here, the brewer did not consider open fermentation worthwhile for this beer.

### [Impact Fermentation Volume Has On An American Light Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2022/08/01/exbeeriment-impact-fermentation-volume-has-on-an-american-light-lager/)

**Date:** 2022-08-01  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.002

#### 1. Purpose

Compare the same American Light Lager recipe fermented at homebrew scale in a 5 gallon/19 liter vessel with a commercially produced 5 barrel batch at Tarantula Hill Brewing.

#### 2. Results

Fourteen tasters identified the different sample when 12 were required, producing a significant result (p=0.002). Among those correct tasters, 11 preferred the 5 barrel beer, 2 preferred the 5 gallon beer, and 1 had no preference. The brewer identified the difference in 4 of 5 trials and perceived the commercial version as drier and more bitter, while the homebrew version seemed slightly sweeter.

#### 3. Discussion

The article treats the result as support for the idea that production scale can affect beer character, potentially through hydrostatic pressure and yeast performance. However, the comparison also changed equipment, packaging process, cooling behaviour, yeast source, and other scale-dependent factors, so fermentation volume was not isolated. The finding establishes that these homebrew and commercial implementations differed, not that hydrostatic pressure alone caused the difference.

### [Impact Open Fermentation Has On Czech Premium Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2020/06/22/the-impact-of-open-fermentation-on-pale-lager-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-06-22  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.44

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Czech Premium Lager fermented in a sealed vessel with the same beer fermented in an open vessel, testing whether the small pressure and oxygen-exposure differences alter a clean lager fermentation.

#### 2. Results

Because normal group data collection was restricted, the brewer completed 10 semi-blind triangle tests. He identified the unique sample 4 times, below the 7 required for significance (p=0.44), and could not reliably distinguish the open and closed beers.

#### 3. Discussion

The article contrasts this result with the significant British Golden Ale open-fermentation trial and proposes that yeast character may be important. A clean lager strain may produce less obvious changes from the small difference between an airlocked and open vessel. The brewer found no practical sensory reason to use open fermentation for this pale lager.

### [Impact Of Open Fermentation On A High OG Saison](https://brulosophy.com/2020/02/17/impact-of-open-fermentation-on-a-high-og-saison-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-02-17  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.021

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a 1.078 OG Saison fermented in a closed vessel with the same wort fermented openly, focusing on claims that reduced backpressure or greater oxygen access prevents Saison yeast from stalling.

#### 2. Results

Twelve tasters identified the odd sample when 11 were required, producing a significant result (p=0.021). Among the correct tasters, 6 preferred the closed beer, 3 preferred the open beer, 1 had no preference, and 2 reported no perceived difference. The brewer identified the odd beer in 4 of 5 trials. Both batches attenuated well, so open fermentation was not needed to prevent a stall in this trial.

#### 3. Discussion

The article concludes that the fermentation environment produced a perceptible difference but does not establish whether oxygen availability, small pressure differences, volatile loss, or another factor caused it. Preference leaned toward the closed beer, and the author did not adopt open fermentation as a routine practice, though he considered it a possible troubleshooting method for brewers who repeatedly experience Saison stalls.

### [Impact Fermentation Volume Has On A Barleywine](https://brulosophy.com/2019/04/29/the-impact-fermentation-volume-has-on-beer-character-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2019-04-29  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.99

#### 1. Purpose

Split one 1.110 OG Barleywine wort between 3 gallon/12 liter and 6 gallon/23 liter glass carboys, using proportionally matched pitch rates and similar vessel geometry to test fermentation volume itself.

#### 2. Results

Both batches finished at 1.019. Of 26 tasters, only 4 identified the unique sample when 14 were required (p=0.99). The brewer selected the odd beer once in 5 trials and found the beers identical in side-by-side tasting.

#### 3. Discussion

Within this twofold homebrew-scale volume difference, the article found no evidence that fermentation volume affected the Barleywine. It emphasizes that the result does not address the much greater hydrostatic pressures of tall commercial tanks. Combined with earlier headspace trials, it suggests that modest batch-size changes can produce equally acceptable beer when pitch rate and process are controlled.

### [Impact Increased Headspace During Fermentation When Volume Is Consistent In A Hazy IPA](https://brulosophy.com/2019/02/18/impact-of-increased-headspace-during-fermentation-when-beer-volume-is-consistent-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2019-02-18  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.89

#### 1. Purpose

Ferment equal 4.25 gallon/16 liter volumes of Hazy IPA in 5 gallon/19 liter and 6.5 gallon/25 liter carboys, creating about 1.5 gallons/5.7 liters of additional headspace while holding beer volume constant.

#### 2. Results

Both beers finished at 1.014. Of 21 tasters, 5 identified the odd sample, well below the 12 required for significance (p=0.89). The brewer also could not distinguish them consistently and described the beers as identical.

#### 3. Discussion

The article found no perceptible effect from this headspace difference and notes that an earlier significant headspace trial also changed beer volume. Possible explanations include the smaller headspace contrast in this test or masking by the strongly hopped beer. It therefore rejects a firm conclusion and supports only that this particular amount of extra headspace was not detectably harmful.

### [Impact Open Fermentation Has On A Kettle Sour Beer](https://brulosophy.com/2018/04/16/the-impact-of-open-fermentation-on-kettle-sour-beer-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2018-04-16  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.97

#### 1. Purpose

Compare kettle wort soured with Lactobacillus plantarum in a sealed vessel with the same wort soured in an open vessel, then finish both beers identically.

#### 2. Results

The sealed wort dropped slightly more in gravity during souring, and the open batch produced a notably unpleasant smell during the subsequent boil. However, only 3 tasters identified the odd finished beer when 10 were required (p=0.97). The brewer identified it 2 of 5 times and found the objectionable open-souring aroma absent after fermentation.

#### 3. Discussion

The article concludes that exposure during the souring phase did not create a reliably perceptible difference in the finished beer, despite obvious process-stage aroma differences. It explicitly limits the finding to kettle souring with the known L. plantarum culture used here and warns against extending it to spontaneous souring or other microorganisms.

### [Impact Open Fermentation Has On A British Golden Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2018/03/19/the-impact-of-open-fermentation-on-beer-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2018-03-19  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.03

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a British Golden Ale fermented in a closed keg with the same wort fermented under a fabric cover in an open vessel, using a characterful English yeast.

#### 2. Results

Thirteen tasters identified the odd sample, exactly meeting the significance threshold (p=0.03). The brewer identified the difference in 4 of 5 semi-blind trials, although he considered it less dramatic than expected and found no evidence that the open beer was contaminated.

#### 3. Discussion

The article treats the significant result as evidence that open fermentation can alter a yeast-driven British ale, potentially through pressure, oxygen availability, or ester expression. It does not claim the open beer was superior, and the author did not adopt the method routinely because the change was modest and open handling introduces additional risk.

### [Impact Increased Headspace During Fermentation Has On A Strong Bitter](https://brulosophy.com/2017/03/13/the-impact-of-increased-headspace-during-fermentation-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-03-13  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.04

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Strong Bitter fermented with a normal, low amount of headspace with the same wort fermented with a much larger amount of headspace, testing whether vessel dimensions and oxygen exposure explain earlier fermenter-comparison results.

#### 2. Results

Twenty tasters participated. Eleven identified the high-headspace sample, exactly reaching significance (p=0.038). Eight of the correct tasters preferred the low-headspace beer, 2 preferred the high-headspace beer, and 1 had no preference. The brewer himself could not distinguish the beers in repeated blind trials.

#### 3. Discussion

The article interprets the result as evidence that headspace or a correlated vessel-dimension effect may influence beer, with oxygen exposure proposed as the leading explanation. Because vessel geometry and hydrostatic conditions also changed, oxygen was not isolated. Later headspace trials did not reproduce the result, so this should be treated as one positive experiment rather than a settled rule.

## Mash and wort-chilling equipment

### [Immersion Chiller vs. Plate Chiller In A German Pils](https://brulosophy.com/2018/01/29/immersion-chiller-vs-plate-chiller-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2018-01-29  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.41

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a German Pils chilled with a copper immersion chiller against the same recipe chilled through a stainless-steel plate chiller.

#### 2. Results

Both worts reached 1.051 OG and both beers finished at 1.011. Of 24 tasters, 9 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 13 required for significance (p=0.41). The brewer was correct in 4 of 12 semi-blind trials and found the beers perceptibly identical.

#### 3. Discussion

The different chilling devices did not create a reliably detectable sensory difference in this pale lager. The comparison also changed copper contact, cold-break destination, and the time portions of the wort remained hot, so a significant result would not have identified a single mechanism. Equipment choice can reasonably be driven by batch size, cleaning, clogging risk, and workflow.

### [Stainless Steel vs. Converted Plastic Cooler Mash Tun In A Vienna Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2017/04/24/stainless-steel-vs-converted-plastic-cooler-mash-tun-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-04-24  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.70

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Vienna Lager mashed in an insulated stainless-steel mash tun against the same recipe mashed in a well-used converted plastic cooler.

#### 2. Results

The mashes produced matching wort gravities and both beers finished at 1.012. Only 6 of 20 tasters identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.70).

#### 3. Discussion

Mash-tun construction did not create a reliable sensory difference when mash temperature, pH, volume, and the remaining process were closely matched. The result supports choosing between these vessels primarily on heat retention, durability, cleaning, cost, and convenience rather than expecting a clear flavour difference.

### [Chilling Wort By Stirring vs. Automation](https://brulosophy.com/2014/12/19/wort-chiller-challenge-elbow-grease-vs-automation-xbmt-results/)

**Date:** 2014-12-19  
**Master-index p-value:** N/A

#### 1. Purpose

Compare active manual agitation of an immersion chiller and wort against automated whirlpool recirculation using a pump and whirlpool arm.

#### 2. Results

This equipment trial compared chilling performance rather than finished-beer sensory data. Manual agitation cooled 10 gallons from boiling to 71°F/22°C in 11 minutes 16 seconds, while automated recirculation took 13 minutes 52 seconds. The automated setup also required additional setup and cleaning.

#### 3. Discussion

Manual agitation was about two and a half minutes faster, while automation eliminated the need to stir continuously. The article presents a workflow tradeoff rather than a quality conclusion: manual chilling minimizes equipment and cleanup, whereas pump-driven recirculation reduces physical effort and allows the brewer to attend to other tasks.

## Hop containment and mechanical filtration equipment

### [Impact Filtering Kettle Hops With A Stainless Mesh Filter Has On An International Pale Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2023/11/27/exbeeriment-impact-filtering-kettle-hops-with-a-stainless-mesh-filter-has-on-an-international-pale-lager/)

**Date:** 2023-11-27  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.52

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an International Pale Lager whose kettle hops were contained in a stainless mesh filter against the same recipe with hops added loose to the wort.

#### 2. Results

The filtered and loose-hop beers finished at 1.014 and 1.013. Of 20 tasters, 7 identified the odd sample, below the 11 required for significance (p=0.52). The brewer was correct in only 2 of 5 semi-blind trials.

#### 3. Discussion

The stainless filter simplified transfer without producing a reliably detectable loss of hop character at this modest hopping rate. The result does not establish that every filter size, hop load, or wort-flow pattern is neutral, particularly when a basket is heavily packed.

### [Kettle Hops: Stainless Hop Spider vs. Loose Additions In An American IPA](https://brulosophy.com/2019/09/16/kettle-hops-stainless-hop-spider-vs-loose-additions-exbeeriment-results/)

### [Impact Dry Hopping In A Stainless Mesh Filter Has On An American Amber Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2019/03/11/impact-of-stainless-mesh-filter-on-dry-hop-character-exbeeriment-results/)

Compare beer dry hopped with pellets contained in stainless mesh filters against the same beer dry hopped with loose pellets.

Both beers finished at 1.014. Only 5 of 25 tasters identified the odd sample, far below the 13 required for significance (p=0.95).

#### 3. Discussion

Containing the dry hops in stainless filters did not produce a reliably detectable reduction in hop aroma or flavour. The filters may still improve closed-transfer reliability and yeast harvesting, although capacity and hop expansion must be considered to avoid restricting contact.

### [Impact Mechanical Filtration Has On A Strong Bitter](https://brulosophy.com/2018/06/04/the-impact-of-mechanical-filtration-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2018-06-04  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.99

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Strong Bitter run through a plate filter at packaging against an unfiltered portion of the same beer.

#### 2. Results

Of 27 tasters, only 4 identified the filtered beer, far below the 14 required for significance (article p=0.992; master index rounded to 0.99). The brewer could not reliably distinguish the beers.
