# Brülosophy exBEERiments: System-Level and Multi-Variable Tests

**Experiments included:** 14

**Source index:** https://brulosophy.com/projects/exbeeriments/

**Prepared:** 31 July 2026

## About this file

This catalogue contains concise, original paraphrases of individual Brülosophy articles that compare complete brewing approaches or deliberately change more than one important variable at once. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct link to its source article and three sections: purpose, results, and discussion.

**Scope:** Included experiments compare stacked variables, bundled Short & Shoddy practices, full low-oxygen brewing against a conventional process, traditional versus accelerated lager production, combined yeast-and-temperature strategies, the same recipe brewed on different systems, a homebrewed version against its commercial target, and extract versus all-grain production. These are system-level comparisons because the treatment is a complete method or because multiple meaningful variables differ deliberately.

**Exclusions:** Ordinary single-variable experiments were excluded even when the tested change had effects at several brewing stages. Short & Shoddy recipe articles without a matched control beer were also excluded because they evaluate a beer made by an abbreviated process rather than directly comparing two systems.

**Interpretive caution:** A multi-variable experiment can determine whether two complete approaches produced distinguishable beer, but it cannot identify which changed component caused the result. Conversely, a non-significant result does not establish that every changed variable was individually unimportant or that the systems were chemically equivalent.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better or quantify the practical size of the difference. A non-significant result means that the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest experiments and several pandemic-era trials did not use the later standardized independent-panel format. Those entries describe the actual informal, comparative, or repeated single-brewer method rather than presenting it as equivalent evidence.

The p-value field follows Brülosophy’s master index. Where the linked article reports a more precise value or uses a different test design, that detail is stated in the results section.

## Stacked and bundled brewing-process comparisons

### [Stacked Variables: Impact Pressurized Fermentation and Temperature Have On A Festbier](https://brulosophy.com/2025/04/21/exbeeriment-stacked-variables-impact-pressurized-fermentation-and-temperature-have-on-a-festbier/)

**Date:** 2025-04-21  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.52

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Festbier fermented warm at 68°F/20°C under 15 psi with the same beer fermented cool at 50°F/10°C without pressure.

#### 2. Results

Both batches started at 1.059 and finished at 1.012, although the warm, pressurized fermentation completed substantially faster. Of 26 tasters, 9 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 14 required for significance (p=0.52). The brewer was correct in only 2 of 5 semi-blind trials and considered the beers essentially identical.

#### 3. Discussion

The complete warm-and-pressurized treatment did not produce a reliably detectable difference from conventional cool, unpressurized fermentation in this Festbier. The result does not show that pressure suppressed warm-fermentation esters, because temperature and pressure changed together. In light of related single-variable trials, the author considered the strain’s tolerance of both conditions a more plausible explanation and retained pressure primarily for convenience and CO2 handling.

### [Stacked Variables: Impact Reduced Mash & Boil Times Have On An American Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2023/05/22/exbeeriment-stacked-variables-impact-reduced-mash-boil-times-have-on-an-american-stout/)

**Date:** 2023-05-22  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.25

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Stout made with a 30-minute mash and 30-minute boil against the same recipe made with conventional 60-minute mash and boil steps.

#### 2. Results

Both worts reached an original gravity of 1.049, and the beers finished at 1.011 and 1.012. Of 24 tasters, 10 identified the odd sample, below the 13 required for significance (p=0.25). The brewer selected the unique beer in only 2 of 5 semi-blind attempts and perceived the beers as identical.

#### 3. Discussion

Halving both major hot-side time commitments did not create a reliably detectable difference in this roast-forward beer. Because mash and boil duration were shortened together, the experiment cannot establish that either change would have been neutral on its own. The recipe’s strong roast character may also have concealed smaller differences that could be more apparent in a delicate style.

### [Traditional vs. Short & Shoddy Brewing Process In A Blonde Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2017/06/26/traditional-vs-short-shoddy-brewing-process-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-06-26  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.01

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Blonde Ale made with a conventional 60-minute mash and boil, a yeast starter, and controlled fermentation at 66°F/19°C against one made with 30-minute mash and boil steps, a direct single-pack pitch, and ambient fermentation at 72°F/22°C.

#### 2. Results

The traditional and abbreviated batches started at 1.045 and 1.044 and finished at 1.011 and 1.010. Thirteen of 22 tasters identified the odd sample, exceeding the 12 required for significance; the article reports p=0.012 while the master index rounds the value to 0.01. Six correct tasters preferred the traditional beer, 2 preferred the Short & Shoddy beer, 4 had no preference, and 1 reported no perceived difference. The brewer was correct in 2 of 3 attempts and found the traditional beer cleaner and crisper, although both beers were considered good.

#### 3. Discussion

The bundled abbreviated process produced a detectable change, but the trial cannot determine whether mash duration, boil duration, pitch rate, fermentation temperature, or their interaction was responsible. The difference was not described as a severe quality failure, and many correct tasters had no preference. The practical conclusion is therefore that cutting several corners at once changed the beer but still produced an acceptable result in this recipe.

### [The LODO Effect: Impact Low Oxygen Brewing Has On A Munich Helles](https://brulosophy.com/2017/04/10/the-lodo-effect-evaluating-the-low-oxygen-brewing-method-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2017-04-10  
**Master-index p-value:** < 0.0001

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a pale German-style beer produced using a comprehensive low-oxygen brewing protocol against the same recipe made with the brewer’s standard hot-side process.

#### 2. Results

The low-oxygen treatment incorporated several interventions, including oxygen-scavenging and oxygen-reduction practices throughout wort production. Twenty-five of 38 tasters identified the low-oxygen beer, producing a highly significant result; the article reports p=0.00004. The treatments also produced materially different original gravities of approximately 1.035 and 1.044.

#### 3. Discussion

The complete low-oxygen method produced distinguishable beer, but this was not a clean test of oxygen exposure alone. Sulfite use, water preparation, grain handling, mash mechanics, transfer practices, and unequal extraction all formed part of the treatment. The result supports the conclusion that the two complete processes differed, not that any individual LODO intervention caused the sensory outcome.

## Accelerated lager and combined fermentation strategies

### [Impact Fermentation Temperature Has When Using Proper Yeast Pitch Rates In A Munich Helles](https://brulosophy.com/2024/01/01/exbeeriment-impact-fermentation-temperature-has-when-using-proper-yeast-pitch-rates-in-a-munich-helles/)

**Date:** 2024-01-01  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.22

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Munich Helles fermented cool using a conventional lager-rate pitch against the same wort fermented warm using a smaller ale-rate pitch.

#### 2. Results

Eight tasters identified the odd sample, below the 10 required for significance (p=0.22). The brewer nevertheless selected the unique beer in all 5 semi-blind trials. He described the warm, lower-pitch-rate beer as sharper, less sulfurous, and slightly fruity, while the cool, higher-pitch-rate beer seemed softer and carried light sulfur.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel did not reliably distinguish the complete traditional and accelerated fermentation treatments, but the brewer’s repeated success suggests the difference may have been perceptible to some individuals. Because pitch rate and temperature changed together, the experiment cannot isolate either factor. It tests two internally coherent fermentation strategies rather than the independent effect of temperature.

### [Yeast Comparison: Omega Yeast OYL-071 Lutra Kveik vs. Saflager W-34/70 In A German Pils - The Brü Club xBmt](https://brulosophy.com/2022/03/03/the-bru-club-xbmt-series-yeast-comparison-omega-yeast-oyl-071-lutra-kveik-vs-saflager-w-34-70-in-a-german-pils/)

**Date:** 2022-03-03  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.053

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a German Pils fermented rapidly at 66°F/19°C with Omega OYL-071 Lutra Kveik against the same wort fermented conventionally at 52°F/11°C with Saflager W-34/70.

#### 2. Results

Lutra completed fermentation within a few days, whereas the W-34/70 batch required nearly two weeks. Of 51 tasters, 23 correctly identified the odd sample, one fewer than the 24 required for significance (p=0.053).

#### 3. Discussion

The result narrowly missed the conventional significance threshold and should not be represented as proof that the beers were equivalent. It nevertheless suggests that the warm-kveik approach produced a German Pils broadly similar to the traditional lager process while substantially reducing fermentation time. Because both yeast strain and temperature changed, their separate contributions cannot be determined.

### [Lager Fermentation: Traditional vs. Quick Lager Method With Imperial Yeast L09 Que Bueno](https://brulosophy.com/2020/12/21/lager-fermentation-traditional-vs-quick-lager-method-with-imperial-yeast-l09-que-bueno-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2020-12-21  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.003

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a pale lager fermented continuously at 50°F/10°C with one begun at the same temperature and then raised to 56°F/13°C and 64°F/18°C early in fermentation.

#### 2. Results

The continuously cool beer finished at 1.015, while the quick-lager beer reached 1.013. Because pandemic restrictions prevented a normal panel, the brewer completed 10 semi-blind triangle tests and selected the unique sample 8 times, producing p=0.003. He found the accelerated beer noticeably more sulfurous and preferred the cleaner traditional version.

#### 3. Discussion

The accelerated schedule created a detectable difference for Imperial Yeast L09 Que Bueno in this beer. The sulfur might have diminished with additional conditioning, so the test does not establish a permanent quality disadvantage. More importantly, the evidence came from repeated trials by one informed brewer rather than an independent panel and is therefore less generalizable than a standard exBEERiment.

### [Lager Methods: Traditional vs. Quick Fermentation In A German Helles Exportbier](https://brulosophy.com/2016/09/19/lager-methods-pt-1-traditional-vs-quick-fermentation-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2016-09-19  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.01

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a German Helles Exportbier produced with a traditional cool fermentation and extended maturation schedule against Brülosophy’s accelerated lager method, which raises fermentation temperature as attenuation progresses and reaches serving condition much sooner.

#### 2. Results

The batches began at the same original gravity and reached the same final gravity. Seventeen of 31 tasters identified the odd sample, exceeding the 16 required for significance (p=0.01). Eleven correct tasters preferred the traditionally fermented beer, 4 preferred the quick lager, and 2 had no preference. The brewer consistently distinguished the samples but preferred the quick version, perceiving more white-grape character in the traditional beer and a light sulfur note in the accelerated one.

#### 3. Discussion

The full fermentation and maturation schedules produced a detectable difference, although the beers were repeatedly described as broadly similar. Preference was divided and the brewer’s preference ran against the majority of correct tasters, so the result does not establish that the traditional method was objectively superior. Brewing date, total turnaround time, temperature schedule, and maturation history form one combined treatment.

### [Yeast Comparison: Saflager W-34/70 vs. WLP029 German Ale In A Märzen](https://brulosophy.com/2015/04/13/lager-fermentation-traditional-yeast-vs-hybrid-yeast-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2015-04-13  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.25

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Märzen produced by cool fermentation with Saflager W-34/70 against the same wort fermented somewhat warmer with the hybrid White Labs WLP029 German Ale/Kölsch strain.

#### 2. Results

Seventeen tasters participated, and 7 identified the odd sample, below the 10 required for significance (p=0.25). The brewer perceived the WLP029 beer as more malt-forward and the W-34/70 beer as crisper, cleaner, slightly hoppier, and more conventionally lager-like, but had little overall preference.

#### 3. Discussion

The panel did not reliably distinguish the traditional lager process from the faster hybrid-yeast approach in this Märzen. The experiment supports WLP029 as a practical route to a similar result under these conditions, but yeast strain, fermentation temperature, and turnaround strategy all changed together. It therefore cannot establish that the cultures themselves were interchangeable.

## Recipe replication and brewer-system effects

### [Doppelgänger: Two Hearted Ale From Bell’s Brewery](https://brulosophy.com/2019/04/22/doppelganger-two-hearted-ale-from-bells-brewery-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2019-04-22  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.002

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a freshly packaged commercial Bell’s Two Hearted Ale with a homebrewed version made from an official Bell’s General Store recipe kit.

#### 2. Results

Seventeen tasters participated, and 12 identified the unique sample, exceeding the 10 required for significance (p=0.002). Of the correct tasters, 3 preferred the commercial beer, 3 preferred the homebrew, and 6 had no preference. The brewer identified the samples in all 3 personal trials, describing the commercial beer as richer and maltier and the homebrew as crisper, drier, and more fruit-forward.

#### 3. Discussion

The kit-based homebrew shared recognizable features with Two Hearted Ale but was not a sensory duplicate. The comparison changed brewery scale, brewhouse and fermentation equipment, water treatment, yeast, packaging, and other process details. The author considered yeast choice particularly important, because the homebrew used Imperial A07 Flagship rather than Bell’s house culture, but the design cannot assign the difference to yeast alone.

### [A Brewer’s Thumbprint: Investigating the Idiosyncrasies of Brewing](https://brulosophy.com/2014/11/27/a-brewers-thumbprint-investigating-the-idiosyncrasies-of-brewing-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2014-11-27  
**Master-index p-value:** N/A

#### 1. Purpose

Determine how similar five Pale Ales would be when five homebrewers used the same supplied ingredients and broad recipe targets but brewed on their own systems with their normal methods.

#### 2. Results

The brewers used materially different water, mash and lautering systems, chilling methods, yeast preparation, fermentation schedules, fining practices, and packaging routines. Their original gravities ranged from approximately 1.046 to 1.061, producing beers from about 4.7% to 6.6% ABV. The tasting was an informal side-by-side evaluation rather than a randomized triangle test, so no inferential p-value was calculated. The beers shared recognizable ingredient-derived traits but were described as plainly different in aroma, bitterness, clarity, flavour, and alcohol expression.

#### 3. Discussion

The experiment illustrates how a recipe is only one part of a brewing system. Brewer decisions, equipment, efficiency, water, yeast handling, oxygen exposure, fermentation, and conditioning jointly shaped the finished beers. Because every brewer’s process differed in several ways and the tasting was informal, the trial cannot identify which factors mattered most or quantify how reproducible each difference would be.

## Extract versus all-grain system comparisons

### [Extract vs. All-Grain American Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2021/02/01/extract-vs-all-grain-american-stout-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2021-02-01  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.0004

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Stout made with dry malt extract and steeped specialty grains against a matched all-grain kit using the same specialty grains, hops, and yeast.

#### 2. Results

The extract and all-grain worts began at 1.051 and 1.052 and both beers finished at 1.012. Pandemic restrictions led the brewer to complete 10 semi-blind triangle tests rather than use an independent panel; he identified the unique sample 9 times, producing p=0.0004. He found the beers surprisingly similar but perceived a deeper nutty or pecan-like aroma in the all-grain version and enjoyed both.

#### 3. Discussion

The author could distinguish the two production systems, but this does not establish that all-grain beer was categorically better. Extract composition, the water used when manufacturing the extract, steeping versus mashing, and broader wort-production differences were inseparable. The result is strongest as evidence that these matched kits produced different beer, not that malt extract necessarily creates a universal flavour defect.

### [Extract vs. All Grain: Comparing American IPA Recipe Kits](https://brulosophy.com/2016/01/04/extract-vs-all-grain-pt-2-comparing-recipe-kits-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2016-01-04  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.02

#### 1. Purpose

Compare extract and all-grain versions of matching American IPA recipe kits intended to produce substantially similar beer.

#### 2. Results

The extract beer finished at 1.019, while the all-grain version reached 1.012 and appeared lighter. Exactly 11 of 20 tasters identified the odd sample, meeting the significance requirement (p=0.020). Four correct tasters preferred the all-grain beer, 2 preferred the extract beer, 3 reported no difference, and 2 perceived a difference without a preference. The brewer repeatedly distinguished the beers, finding the extract version richer and more caramel-like and the all-grain beer cleaner, toastier, and more hop-forward.

#### 3. Discussion

The matched kits did not produce interchangeable beer, but the seven-point final-gravity difference means attenuation, sweetness, body, and balance were part of the treatment effect. Extract manufacture, recipe formulation, wort-production method, and timing also differed. The experiment supports the practical conclusion that these two complete kit systems produced distinguishable results, not that one method is universally superior.

### [Extract vs. All Grain In A Blonde Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2014/07/21/extract-vs-all-grain-exbeeriment-results/)

**Date:** 2014-07-21  
**Master-index p-value:** N/A

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Blonde Ale made with 100% Maris Otter liquid malt extract against an all-grain version designed with Maris Otter malt to reach the same original gravity.

#### 2. Results

The beers reached the same original gravity and finished at 1.011 and 1.010, but the extract beer was visibly darker. This early experiment used an open side-by-side survey of 14 people rather than a triangle test. Eight tasters preferred the extract beer overall, and 8 correctly selected it after being told that one beer contained extract. Tasters commonly described the extract beer as fuller, sweeter, more caramel-like, and more bitter, while the all-grain beer was considered lighter, crisper, and more hop-forward.

#### 3. Discussion

The comparison produced clear descriptive differences, but its non-randomized early format cannot support a conventional significance claim. Appearance may have influenced identification, and the treatments differed in ingredient processing, wort production, mash procedure, and possibly mineral contribution from the manufactured extract. Preference favoured the extract beer in this small panel, showing that difference should not be confused with inferiority.
