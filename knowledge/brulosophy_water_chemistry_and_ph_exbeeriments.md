# Brülosophy exBEERiments: Water Chemistry and pH

**Experiments included:** 31

**Source index:** https://brulosophy.com/projects/exbeeriments/

**Prepared:** 29 July 2026

## About this file

This catalogue indexes Brülosophy experiments in which the tested variable was brewing-water mineral content, water source, deliberate pH adjustment, or acidification. It is not a copy of the article text. Every experiment includes a direct link to its source article and three sections: purpose, results, and discussion.

**Scope:** Included experiments test mash, boil, post-boil, or finished-beverage pH; acid source or acidification timing; sulfate, chloride, sodium, calcium, gypsum, table salt, or complete mineral profiles; reverse-osmosis, tap, untreated, or water-heater water; and mineral additions made after fermentation. Hop-water experiments are included only when the tested variable is acidification or mineral chemistry. Water-temperature, mash-method, dissolved-oxygen, antioxidant, and novelty water-substitution experiments were excluded when chemistry or pH was not the isolated variable.

**Statistical caution:** A significant triangle test supports the conclusion that the tested samples were distinguishable to that panel under those conditions. It does not show which treatment was better or identify the causal ions, acids, or flavour compounds. A non-significant result means that the experiment failed to detect a difference; it is not proof that the treatments are equivalent.

Brülosophy’s earliest experiments did not always use the later standardised triangle-test format. Entries therefore describe the method actually reported rather than inventing panel details that are not present in the source.

The p-value field reproduces the value shown in Brülosophy’s master index. Where the underlying article provides a more precise value or materially disagrees with the index title, that discrepancy is stated in the results section.

## Mash pH level and mash-adjustment methods

### [Water Chemistry: Impact Increasing Mash pH With Slaked Lime Has On An Oatmeal Stout](https://brulosophy.com/2025/02/24/exbeeriment-water-chemistry-impact-increasing-mash-ph-with-slaked-lime-has-on-an-oatmeal-stout/)

**Date:** 2025-02-24  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.46

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an oatmeal stout mashed at approximately pH 5.2 against an otherwise matched batch whose mash pH was raised to approximately 5.6 with slaked lime.

#### 2. Results

Twenty-two tasters participated, and 8 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 12 required for significance (p=0.46). The panel therefore did not reliably distinguish the higher-pH stout from the lower-pH version.

#### 3. Discussion

Raising mash pH with slaked lime did not produce a detectable sensory difference in this oatmeal stout. The result applies to the tested pH range and recipe; it does not show that mash pH is unimportant at more extreme values or in paler, less strongly flavoured beers.

## Boil, post-boil and beverage acidification

### [Impact Pre-Boil Acidification Has On A Czech Amber Lager](https://brulosophy.com/2026/02/09/exbeeriment-impact-pre-boil-acidification-has-on-a-czech-amber-lager/)

**Date:** 2026-02-09  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.58

#### 1. Purpose

Compare a Czech Amber Lager acidified with lactic acid before the boil against an otherwise matched batch receiving the same acid dose after the boil.

#### 2. Results

The pre-boil addition reduced wort pH from approximately 5.46 to 5.13, while the other batch received the same 4.5 mL dose of 88% lactic acid after boiling. The master index reports p=0.58, so the panel did not reliably distinguish the beers.

Moving the same acid addition from post-boil to pre-boil did not produce a detectable finished-beer difference in this recipe. The treatments differed in boil pH and acid exposure time, but not in acid type or nominal dose.

## Water source and untreated water

### [Reverse Osmosis (RO) vs. Tap Water In An American Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2022/08/22/exbeeriment-reverse-osmosis-ro-vs-tap-water-in-an-american-pale-ale/)

**Date:** 2022-08-22  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.90

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Pale Ale brewed with reverse-osmosis water against the same recipe brewed with tap water, with both sources adjusted toward the same target profile.

#### 2. Results

Of 29 tasters, 7 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 15 required for significance (p=0.90). The RO-water beer appeared clearer, but the brewer considered the aroma, flavour, and mouthfeel perceptibly identical.

Once both sources were adjusted toward the same profile, the panel did not reliably detect whether the starting water was RO or tap. This supports the importance of final chemistry over source label in this case, but it does not cover tap water containing problematic chlorine, alkalinity, metals, or other contaminants.

## Post-fermentation and single-mineral additions

### [Water Chemistry: Impact Post-Fermentation Gypsum Addition Has On An American Pale Ale](https://brulosophy.com/2022/11/14/exbeeriment-water-chemistry-impact-post-fermentation-gypsum-addition-has-on-an-american-pale-ale/)

**Date:** 2022-11-14  
**Master-index p-value:** 0.95

#### 1. Purpose

Compare an American Pale Ale served with its original mineral profile against the same finished beer dosed with gypsum after fermentation.

#### 2. Results

The gypsum dose increased the estimated profile from about 92 ppm calcium and 152 ppm sulfate to 155 ppm calcium and 306 ppm sulfate, with chloride unchanged near 49 ppm. Of 21 tasters, 4 correctly identified the odd sample, below the 12 required for significance (p=0.95).

#### 3. Discussion

A large post-fermentation sulfate increase was not reliably detected in this beer. Dissolution, distribution, serving conditions, and the existing hop bitterness may have limited the sensory effect, so the result should not be treated as proof that gypsum additions are flavourless.

## General limitations

These experiments are useful controlled comparisons, but most involve one recipe, one brew system, one execution of each treatment, and modest sensory panels. Water chemistry effects depend on the complete ion profile, alkalinity, grist, acid strength and dose, pH measurement temperature and calibration, mash thickness, hopping, yeast, fermentation, carbonation, and serving conditions. A result from one beer should therefore be treated as evidence for that specific comparison, not as a universal brewing rule.

Several experiments change more than one chemically related variable at once. Adding gypsum changes both calcium and sulfate; table salt changes sodium and chloride; changing a sulfate-to-chloride ratio may also change total mineral load; and acidulated malt changes both acidification and a portion of the grist. Significant results establish distinguishability, but usually do not isolate the exact chemical cause.

Finally, p-values do not measure the magnitude or practical importance of a difference. Preference data are reported where available, but preference is panel-specific and should not be treated as a general ranking of brewing methods.
